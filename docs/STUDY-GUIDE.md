# NEET PG 2026 — How to use this study material

**Exam: 30 August 2026. Study window: 11–29 August, 19 days.**

This document is the map. Read it once, properly, before you open any of the PDFs —
it will change how you use them.

---

## 1. What this material is, and what it is not

**It is** every previous-year question you have, sorted into topics, with a teaching
chapter written for each topic that is built *from those specific questions*.

**It is not** revision notes. A revision note assumes you already know the topic and
just reminds you of the facts. These chapters teach the concept — mechanism,
classification, clinical correlation — and then show you every way the exam has
actually asked about it. You can learn a topic from scratch here.

**It is also not a textbook, and it is machine-written.** Every chapter ends with a
`Standard textbook reference` section naming specific chapters in the standard books.
That section is an instruction, not decoration. Anything that contradicts what you
already know, and in particular **drug doses, numerical cut-offs, and national
programme figures**, should be checked against the book before you commit it to
memory. You have three weeks; a wrong number learned confidently is worse than a gap.

---

## 2. The goal

Not "cover the syllabus". The goal is: **on 30 August, for every question on the
paper, you have already seen that concept tested at least once.**

That is achievable in 19 days only because the exam repeats itself. The material is
organised entirely around exploiting that.

---

## 3. Where the questions came from — and why it matters

Your question bank is not one exam. It is three, and they are not equally useful:

| Exam | Questions | Years | What it is |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **NEET PG** | 1,460 | 2019–2025 | The exam you are sitting |
| **INI CET** | 265 | 2025, May 2026 | AIIMS/PGI/JIPMER/NIMHANS combined entrance |
| **AIPGMEE** | 9,277 | 2012–2018 | NEET PG's direct predecessor — same exam, renamed in 2019 |

Two things follow from this table, and they are the whole reason the ranking works.

**First: 84% of your questions are from AIPGMEE.** If topics were ranked by how often
they appear, the 2014 paper alone — 2,104 questions — would outvote the last three
real NEET PG papers combined. You would spend your best days studying what was
high-yield twelve years ago.

**Second: the recent papers are small.** NEET PG 2024 contributed 110 questions. A raw
count buries them.

So topics are **not** ranked by count. Each topic is scored on its **share of each
paper it appeared in**, and each paper is weighted by how much it predicts your 2026
exam:

| Paper | Weight |
|---|---:|
| NEET PG 2025 | **0.78** |
| NEET PG 2024 | 0.61 |
| INI CET May 2026 | 0.60 |
| INI CET 2025 | 0.47 |
| NEET PG 2020 | 0.22 |
| AIPGMEE 2014 | **0.02** |

Recent NEET PG and INI CET papers decide the ranking. AIPGMEE cannot outvote them,
but it still breaks ties between topics the recent papers happened not to touch —
which is exactly what you want, because those topics are still examinable.

> **A caveat you should know.** NBE does not release NEET PG papers and AIIMS does not
> release INI CET papers. Every paper from 2025 onward is a memory-based recall
> compiled by students. Questions from those papers are marked
> *"memory-based recall"* under the question box. Treat their answer keys as strong
> evidence, not gospel — if a chapter flags a disagreement, believe the chapter's
> reasoning over the key.

## 4. What the numbers on every chapter mean

Each chapter is headed with something like:

> `Tier A | ~1.8 expected questions | 34 previous-year questions`

**`~1.8 expected questions`** is the important one. It is a literal prediction: *this
topic is worth about 1.8 questions on your 200-question paper.* Across all topics
these sum to roughly 200. So a topic marked `~0.2` is worth about a fifth of one
question — read it if you have time, skip it without guilt if you don't.

**Tier** is your priority band. It is a *ranking* across all 573 topics, not a fixed
score cut-off — with 573 topics sharing 200 expected questions the average topic is
worth about 0.35, so an absolute rule would put almost everything in the bottom band
and defeat the purpose. Tier controls how much was written and when it is scheduled:

| Tier | Where it ranks | Chapter | Read it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Top 20% (~115 topics) | Full teaching chapter, ~2,000 words | Always. This is the exam. |
| **B** | Next 35% (~200 topics) | Standard chapter, ~1,200 words | Always, if you are on schedule |
| **C** | Bottom 45% (~256 topics) | Compact fact sheet, ~500 words | If time allows. **This is what you cut.** |

The two numbers answer different questions. `Tier` tells you *what to read first*;
`expected questions` tells you *how much that topic is actually worth*. A tier-A topic
at 0.9 and a tier-B topic at 0.85 are nearly equivalent in value — the band is a
scheduling convenience, not a cliff.

**`trend`** tells you whether a topic is *rising* (recent papers ask it more than the
old ones did) or *falling*. A rising tier-B topic deserves more of your attention than
a falling tier-A one.

## 5. The files, and the order to open them

### Start here — `NEETPG_00_MASTER_INDEX.pdf`
Your navigation document. Contains the top-100 topics ranked across all subjects, the
full topic list for every subject with its expected-question score, and the paper
weight table. **Read the top-100 list on day 1 and again on day 19.** If you did
nothing else, knowing what is on that list would still move your score.

### Then — `NEETPG_00_CALENDAR.pdf`
The 19-day plan, one page per day, with tick boxes and a page reference for every
topic. See §6 for how it is built and how to recover when you fall behind.

### The bulk — `NEETPG_<Subject>.pdf` (one per subject)
The actual study material. Each has a table of contents, and chapters are ordered
**high-yield first within each section** — so if you stop reading a section halfway,
you have already read the part that matters most.

Every chapter contains:

- **Must know** — the bare facts. If you read nothing else in the chapter, read this.
- **Concept sections** — the actual teaching.
- **Tables** — comparisons. This exam loves comparisons; these are the highest-value
  thing on the page.
- **Mnemonics** — standard ones used in Indian medical teaching, not invented ones.
- **Asked again and again** — concepts that appeared in several different papers,
  detected automatically. **This is the single most predictive section in the
  chapter.** A concept asked in three separate years is the best available evidence it
  will be asked a fourth time.
- **Where students lose marks** — the traps, and any place the source explanation
  disagreed with the correct answer.
- **Standard textbook reference** — see §1. Not decoration.
- **Previous-year questions** — every PYQ for that topic, verbatim. These are printed
  from the original data, never rewritten.

**Answers are not printed next to the questions.** All four options are shown blank,
so a question can actually be attempted. Each topic's answers sit in an **Answer key**
immediately after that topic's questions — a compact marking grid first, then the full
explanations — numbered `Q1.`, `Q2.` from the start of the topic. Attempt the topic's
questions, turn the page, mark them, read the explanations while the topic is still
fresh. If you would rather have them inline, or one key at the very back:

```bash
python3 -m pipeline.render_pdf --all --with-answers
python3 -m pipeline.render_pdf --all --set questions.answerKey.placement=document
```

Some stems refer to a photograph, ECG or micrograph that the source paper lost. Those
carry an orange **"Image not available"** banner, so a question you cannot answer is
visibly the paper's fault rather than yours.

### Read once, early — `docs/ANSWER-KEY-CORRECTIONS.md`
Every place where the study material disagrees with the answer key or explanation
stored against a previous-year question. The corpus is not authoritative — 41% of its
explanations are machine-written, and every paper from 2025 on is a memory-based
recall — so writing these down beats silently resolving them. **Treat them as claims
to verify against your book, not as corrections to trust.** Learning a wrong key
confidently is worse than skipping the question.

### For the last two days — `NEETPG_HIGH_YIELD_ONLY.pdf`
Every tier-A chapter from every subject, in one file, ranked. This is what you carry
on 28–29 August. Nothing else.

## 6. The 19-day plan, and what the minutes mean

### The minutes are measured, not guessed

An earlier version of this calendar estimated study time from a topic's high-yield
score alone — it never looked at how long the chapter was or how many questions it
carried. Cardiac Arrhythmias came out at 23 minutes for 2,058 words, 6 tables, 17
must-know lines and 32 MCQs. That was wrong by more than three times, and it made an
impossible plan look like it fitted.

Every entry is now costed from the material:

```
minutes = words/130 + table_rows x 12s + must_know x 15s + scheduled_questions x 1.5min
```

So each row shows **Read** and **Solve** separately. Cardiac Arrhythmias is 43 minutes:
28 reading, 15 solving. If those numbers are wrong for you, they are one command away
from being right — see §8.

### Only some questions are scheduled

Solving every one of the 10,963 questions would take 274 hours on its own. The calendar
budgets **2,036 of them**: every NEET PG and INI CET question from 2019 onward, plus one
representative per repeat cluster. Those are precisely the questions the ranking says
predict your paper.

The rest stay in the subject PDFs. A question the calendar budgets for is marked
**★ ON THE PLAN** under its box — everything else is there for extra practice on a topic
you know is weak, not for the first pass.

### What does not fit, and why that is stated rather than hidden

At 10 h/day the plan schedules about 178 of the 190 available hours, and **roughly 350
topics do not fit**. The calendar says so explicitly and names them, lowest-yield first.

It is arranged so that what is missing is what the exam asks least:

- **Every tier-A topic is scheduled.** Priority is global, so nothing high-yield is ever
  dropped in favour of something lower.
- Tier C is scheduled as its **Must-know list only** — marked *(Must-know only)* in the
  calendar.
- What falls off the end is the bottom of tier B and C.

If you want more coverage: `--tier-c skip` drops tier C entirely and frees its time for
tier B, `--hours 12` buys about 38 more hours, and `--first-pass-days 17` trades two
revision days for two more study days.

### How to work a day

Each row is one topic with a tick box, a page reference and a time split:

> `☐ Cardiac Arrhythmias & Conduction Disorders | Cardiology | A | 28m | 15m | 10/37 | p41`

1. Open the subject PDF at that page.
2. Read the chapter — concept sections, then the tables. That is the **Read** minutes.
3. Do the **★ ON THE PLAN** questions — 10 of the 37 here. That is the **Solve** minutes.
4. Tick the box.

Four decisions shape the rest, and it is worth knowing what you trade by deviating:

1. **Tier A is scheduled first.** Falling behind therefore costs you the least.
2. **Three or four subjects a day, not one.** Blocked study feels tidier and retains
   worse; the real paper mixes subjects too.
3. **A fifth of each day is revisits** — topics from 3 and 9 days earlier, at a quarter
   of their original time. Not optional padding: it is what stops week one evaporating.
4. **Days 16-19 introduce nothing new.** Two Must-know sweeps, a full 200-question timed
   mock built from NEET PG 2023-2025 and INI CET 2025-2026, then its review.

**If you are more than two days behind by day 10:** stop reading tier C, and for tier B
read only *Must know* and *Asked again and again*. Never skip tier A.

## 7. Bringing doubts back

Every artifact is keyed by two stable ids, and quoting them is the fastest way to get
a precise answer:

- **Topic id** — e.g. `pharmacology.antimicrobial-chemotherapy.antitubercular-drugs`.
  Printed in the topic index and used as the chapter filename.
- **Question id** — e.g. `neetpg-2025-s1-q0042`, `inicet-2026-s1-q0011`. Printed under
  every question box.

So: *"In `pharmacology.…​.antitubercular-drugs`, the Must-know says X but Katzung says
Y — which is right?"* is answerable immediately. "That TB drug question was wrong" is not.

Worth flagging back, in particular:
- anything contradicting your textbook, especially doses and cut-offs
- an answer key you believe is wrong (likely on memory-based 2025–2026 questions)
- a topic that feels mis-scored against your own sense of the exam
- a question tagged to a topic it does not belong in

## 8. Rebuilding any of this

**The written chapters are stored, and re-rendering the PDFs is free.** The material
lives as JSON in `data/topics/chapters/`; `render_pdf.py` is pure reportlab and has
never called a model. So any change to how the PDFs look or what they include is a
re-render, not a regeneration — bigger type, answers inline, tier A only, no question
banks, a blind mock paper. Every one of those is a setting in `config/render.json`.

**See [REGENERATING-PDFS.md](REGENERATING-PDFS.md) for the full list of settings and
some ready-made recipes.** Ask for changes by name; nothing needs to be rewritten.

Everything else is regenerable from the corpus too. From the repository root:

```bash
python3 -m pipeline.taxonomy      --emit | --ingest   # topic taxonomy
python3 -m pipeline.tag_topics    --rules             # free keyword pass
python3 -m pipeline.tag_topics    --emit-packets      # the rest, as work packets
python3 -m pipeline.tag_topics    --ingest
python3 -m pipeline.apply_topics  --dry-run           # inspect before writing
python3 -m pipeline.apply_topics  --commit            # merge topics into the corpus
python3 -m pipeline.build_topic_index                 # scoring and ranking
python3 -m pipeline.chapters      --emit --subject X  # chapter work packets
python3 -m pipeline.chapters      --ingest            # validate and store chapters
python3 -m pipeline.render_pdf    --all --index --high-yield --calendar
python3 -m pipeline.build_calendar --hours 10         # change your daily budget
python3 -m pipeline.render_pdf    --write-config      # list every layout setting
```

The flags you are most likely to want:

```bash
# your real pace — time yourself on day 1 and put the truth in
python3 -m pipeline.build_topic_index --wpm 100 --pyq-minutes 2
python3 -m pipeline.build_calendar --hours 8

# more coverage, less depth
python3 -m pipeline.build_calendar --tier-c skip --first-pass-days 17

# schedule every question rather than the priority subset
python3 -m pipeline.build_topic_index --pyq-scope all
```

Rebuild the calendar PDF afterwards with `python3 -m pipeline.render_pdf --calendar`.
Both steps are plain Python and take seconds. **A plan you actually follow beats an
optimal one you abandon on day three** — if 10 hours a day is fiction, put in the real
number and let the plan tell you honestly what fits.

Every step that writes to the corpus backs it up first (`data/backups/`) and refuses
to write if it would change any field it did not declare, so re-running is safe.
